Overall, I reproduced the original results of the simulation quite well. One important caveat though is that the simulation involves two separate types of randomness, the first in generating the individuals, and the second in generating the experiments. Thus, my true parameter for the generated individuals is different from that of the original creators, causing sizable differences in the numbers. With that being said, I still got similar levels of bias and variance for the logit model estimates and plug in estimates, and the main takeaways from the simulations were the same. It would be extremely weird for me to actually achieve very similar results to their study because of the randomness in generating the individuals, especially for the simulations with smaller amounts of individuals.

The simulation conditions were fair to all methods being compared, as far as I can tell. The conditions had to be of the form: First generate individual conditional outcomes and covariates, then generate assignments. As far as I am aware there was no predecessor which told which specific generating mechanism for conditional outcomes and covariates, as well assignments, would lead to poor performance for the logistic model. The authors admit that the generating mechanism for covariates and latents, and for the rules for determining conditional outcomes were mostly arbitrary, as well as deciding what percentage were assigned to treatment or control. The authors do point out that the performance of the logistic model depends on what percentage is assigned to treatment, and so this does break neutrality slightly. They do also mention that changing the covariate in a different way leads to a much larger bias for the logistic model, so overall it seems as though the experiment was mostly neutral, however they clearly did many more simulations without really giving us the results, so I cannot quite speak to how truthful and neutral they are being. The scenarios were somewhat realistic although idealized. Of course, real world data does not follow these models exactly, but it is somewhat of a standard setting to have the control outcome be based purely off of a latent variable and the treatment outcome based off of a linear combination of the latent and covariate. The specific numbers they chose could appear in a real setting, and the assignment mechanism is exactly what they do in real life controlled experiments.

If I could change the simulation design, I would generate a lot more sets of individuals, to see how the bias depends on the true parameter. I would also try out different outcome thresholds, assignment percentages, and covariate/latent distributions/setups, to see how they impact the performance of the different methods. Of course, the author says that they did this and gives us a brief description of the result, but I would want to actually see them more thoroughly, and see their associated charts.

They only had one chart, so I made my own, but I also remade a chart similar to theirs for comparison. One visualization I made showcased how related the two methods estimates were, something which while obvious the paper did not really mention. One of my visualizations did not reveal anything that the paper missed or undersold, but it provided a graphic which exemplifies and showcases what the authors wrote in words, that the logit estimator was inconsistent and biased, while the plugin was consistent.

Nothing was actually unexpected in the results, the simulations simply showcased the point of the authors paper, and were therefore more or less expected.
